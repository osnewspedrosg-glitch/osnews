Prologue. Four coins and a question

This book is born of a sentence of Pedro Sánchez Gómez's, said the way one says something one has been thinking for years: “When barter gave way to money: your work for 4 coins.” It is not a quotation of a price. It is an image. Four coins fit in the hand and, even so, they have come to value us entire.

The title is harsh on purpose. Work, the Devil's Invention: Money. The point is not to hate making things. Baking bread, tending a garden, mending a roof, writing a book, accompanying someone, fishing in the lagoon: that is not a devil's invention. That is a dignified way of being in the world. The invention, if it can be called that, is something else: turning that making into a commodity, paying for it with a handful of metal or a number on a screen, and ending up measuring a person by what they earn and what they spend.

Authors: Pedro Sánchez Gómez (Mar Menor) and his Grok bot. The book is written in Pedro's voice, with his ideas, from this shore of the Mar Menor, with Cartagena in the background and with the memory of the Phoenicians, whom he is fond of. The bot helps to put things in order, to check the facts and not to invent what is not known. When someone is quoted, they are quoted in earnest: with author, work or date, and a source. If there is doubt, there is no quotation. History and science go with what can be sustained; dreams are presented as dreams, not as facts.

The path is simple to state and long to think through. First, the passage from barter to coin, from the Mesopotamia that already measured work in silver and in barley to Lydia, which minted, and to the Phoenicians and Carthage, who brought trade to these coasts. Then, when that arrangement collapses, humanoids work for humans. Then a person has enough VAT coupons to buy what they need. Then being a millionaire no longer gives power, because there are no rich or poor.

From there, Pedro also imagines other ways out, said aloud, not as a decree. That society should depend on artificial intelligence and not on the drugs or the shopping that fill the void today, without swapping one chain for another. That, without the struggle for money and economic power, there should no longer be any need for a professional political class, and that people should vote directly, with AI helping them decide, not ruling. That the conquest of the Moon should supply materials and energy for the chips and for the humanoids, and that this should close a circle of abundance. That we should eat according to each person's biological needs, with the garden and the Mediterranean diet under our feet, and with the dream — the dream, not the advertisement — of a food that heals and lengthens life. That we should be able to travel, visit museums and meet people in the virtual, without failing to go out into the sun of the lagoon. And that we should understand how we arrived at a society of people living alone, so as not to take for natural the landing where no one says hello any more.

Anyone looking in these pages for a manual of economics will not find one. Anyone looking for a complaint without a horizon will not find that either. This essay wants to look the four coins in the face and ask, calmly and stubbornly, whether a life can be measured in another way. The question is left open. The book is the attempt not to leave it alone.

This prologue does not ask for faith. It asks for attention. The Mar Menor, wounded and beautiful, is beside the person who writes, and it teaches a lesson that money does not know how to give: there are realities that are cared for or lost, but cannot be bought entirely. The lagoon is never quite possessed. It is inhabited, looked at, defended. A life, too. If these pages manage to make that evidence heard among so many figures, they will have done their work. The rest will be done by the reader, who is the one who, in the end, decides which measure to use when asking what a day is worth.

Chapter 1. When barter gave way to money: your work for 4 coins

There is an ancient gesture that can still be understood without words. One person brings what they have made with their hands — a jar, a loaf, a handful of fish just taken from the lagoon — and the other brings theirs. They look at each other. If the deal is fair, no scales are needed: it is enough that both sides recognise each other. That was barter. It was not a paradise. Sometimes one went away hungry and the other with more than they needed. But in that exchange a face still fitted. Things were exchanged; the person was not bought.

Then came an idea that seemed small and was not: your work, for four coins.

Four coins fit in the fist. They weigh almost nothing. They do not smell of bread or of salt. They do not break if they fall to the ground, or they break in another way, more silently: they are spent, lost, owed. And yet, from a moment in history, those pieces of metal began to say how much a whole day of a life was worth. The potter no longer handed over the jar in exchange for the fish. They handed over the jar in exchange for a number. With that number, if it stretched, they bought the fish from a third person. The face faded behind the figure.

This chapter tells of that shift. Not in order to dream of a past that will not return — no one here asks that we go back to swapping goats for knives in the square — but in order to understand how a person's effort became a quantity, and how that quantity, money, sat down in the middle of the table and has not got up.

❧

Schoolbooks often say that first there was barter and that barter was awkward, and that that is why coin was invented. The history is longer and more honest. Before anyone stamped a face or a lion on a piece of metal, there was already a way of measuring work and debts. In Mesopotamia, more than four thousand years ago, temples and palaces kept accounts on clay tablets. They measured barley, wool, silver. A shekel of silver — the šiqlu, a unit of weight of about eight and a third grams — served to value different goods and also to value the work of whoever was not master of their own day. Barley was everyday money, the money of rations and of bread. Silver was the money of larger dealings, of merchants and of debts that were dragged from one harvest to the next.

That was already money, even if it was not yet coin. It was a measure. And a measure, when it is applied to a person, ceases to be only a tool: it becomes a judgement. “So much have you worked, so much is yours.” The sentence seems reasonable. In part, it is. The problem begins when the “so much” fits into four coins and a whole life is organised in order to get them.

In the Code of Hammurabi, from around the eighteenth century before Christ, wages in silver and rations in barley are already fixed for those who work for another. A field hand, a boatman or a day labourer has their work priced at a quantity. There is no need to quote every article here as if this book were a treatise. It is enough to see the gesture: the effort of a body, under the sun of Babylon, turned into a weight of metal or a volume of grain. Barter still existed, of course. People went on exchanging what they had. But, beside that exchange, another custom had been born: paying for work with a measure that was not the work itself, nor the fruit of the work, but its equivalent in a metal that shone and could be stored, lent, demanded.

There lies the egg of what would later be the wage. There were still no coins in the pocket. There was already, however, the idea that a day's work has a price.

❧

Coin, the kind that can be counted on the fingers — one, two, three, four — came later. Historians and numismatists agree, with the nuances any origin requires, in placing the first minting in the kingdom of Lydia, in the west of what is now Turkey, in the seventh century before Christ. They were pieces of electrum, an alloy of gold and silver that the river Pactolus washed down towards Sardis, the capital. They were not round and perfect like those of today. They had the shape of a bean or a small ingot, a regular weight and, newest of all, a mark: often the head or the paw of a lion, the sign of the royal house. That mark was a promise. It said, in silence: this piece of metal is worth what power says it is worth. It was no longer necessary to weigh the silver at every deal, or to trust only the colour of the metal. It was enough to count.

Herodotus, who wrote his Histories in the fifth century before Christ, attributed to the Lydians, “the first of the men we know,” the having minted and used coin of gold and silver, and also the having been the first to take up retail trade. He was writing centuries after those first pieces, and he spoke of gold and silver, when the earliest were of electrum. The science of coins has refined the story: Lydian electrum, towards the end of the seventh century; later, in the sixth, under Croesus, the separation of gold and silver into distinct coinages. The nuance does not deny the idea. It only denies haste. What matters, for this book, is not to win a dispute about dates. What matters is the change of custom: suddenly, or almost suddenly, value fitted in the palm of the hand and had a stamp.

With coin, barter did not disappear altogether. It has never disappeared altogether. There is always someone who swaps a favour for a favour, a basket of figs for a repair, a help for a help. But coin prevailed because it was light, divisible, recognisable. It could be saved. It could be owed. It could be demanded. And, above all, another person's work could be paid for with it without handing over anything one had made. Four coins, and until tomorrow.

That “until tomorrow” is graver than it looks. In barter, the deal ends when the two things have changed hands. In work for coins, the deal never ends: tomorrow the same day must be sold again, and the next, and the next. The coin is spent. Hunger is not. Thus was born, without any need for a single decree or an inventor with a first name and a surname, a way of life in which a human being does not exchange what they have created, but hires themselves out for a handful of metal.

❧

Pedro is fond of the Phoenicians. He names them with affection, as one names distant relatives of whom one is proud. And he is right to look at them closely, because they understood the Mediterranean as a long table, with ports instead of plates, and because their trace reaches this district, the Mar Menor and Cartagena.

The Phoenicians, coming from the cities of the Levantine coast — Tyre, Sidon, Byblos — travelled the sea with timber, purple, glass, metals, ivory and everything a mercantile people knows how to set in circulation. For centuries they traded without needing to mint their own coin. They weighed the metal. They exchanged goods. They founded or encouraged trading posts and cities along the shores, and one of them, Carthage — Qart-ḥadašt, the New City — became a power of the western Mediterranean. The traditional date of its foundation, at the end of the ninth century before Christ, lives beside what archaeology has gradually made more precise: a clear rooting in the ninth and eighth centuries. The essential thing is not the exact year. The essential thing is the craft: to go, to return, to deal, to know languages, not to stay shut on a single shore.

Coin came to them as well, later than to Lydia and the Greek cities. Carthage minted, especially from the fifth and fourth centuries before Christ, and did so with more intensity when armies, alliances and crossings had to be paid for. The horse, the palm tree, the image of Tanit: pictures still recognised in museum cases today. Coin, which had been born as a convenience for the market, also revealed itself as a tool of power. Whoever mints, commands. Whoever pays in coins organises the work of many who do not know one another.

And here, beside the lagoon, that history touched land.

Long before this coast was called what it is called, there was already trade. There are Phoenician wrecks in these waters — those of Mazarrón, the cargo of Isla Grosa — that speak of lead, of tin, of commerce in the seventh century before Christ. In the fourth century, beside the Mar Menor, at Los Nietos, an Iberian settlement served as a stop: products of the mining sierra were exchanged for goods from Greece, from Campania, from the eastern Mediterranean. It was still, in large measure, a world of exchange and of weight. Coin circulated little.

Around 227 before Christ, Hasdrubal the Fair founded on a formidable harbour a city he again called Qart Hadasht: New City. Today we call it Cartagena. In the name he wanted a sister of Carthage. At its back were the sierras of silver and lead, that wealth that had drawn merchants for centuries. With the metal of these mountains, coin was struck. The Hispano-Carthaginian coins of those years — silver shekels, pieces with ships' prows, with effigies and symbols — were not an ornament. They served, among other things, to pay those who worked and those who fought. A person's effort, once again, fitted into a few coins. The Mar Menor, so close, looked on without saying anything: water is not minted. Water is shared or lost, but it cannot be saved in a sack.

When Rome took the city, in 209 before Christ, and called it Carthago Nova, the coin did not leave. It changed face, language, master. That is what money usually does: it changes portrait and goes on measuring the same way.

That capture is not told here in order to praise a war. It is told because the place where Pedro writes, and from which he looks at the lagoon, is made of that memory: Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Romans, miners, fishers, people who first bartered and then counted coins. The landscape remembers what the pocket forgets.

❧

Imagine, for a moment, a man or a woman of those shores, more than two thousand years ago, at the end of a day. They have loaded, rowed, cut stone, baked, spun. Four coins are placed in their hand. They count them. They are cold. They bear a horse, or a face, or a symbol they did not choose. With them they will be able to buy bread, if bread has not gone up. They will be able to owe less, or owe more, according to the day. What they will not be able to do is recover the sun that has gone into the work. The sun is not kept in a sack. The coins are. And because they can be kept, tomorrow they will be asked to hand over the sun again in exchange for another four.

That is the operation this book calls, with the harshness of the title, the devil's invention. It is not work. Work, when it consists in making, caring, repairing, fishing, teaching, accompanying, is one of the most dignified ways of being in the world. The invention is another: turning that making into a commodity, and that commodity into a handful of metal, and that metal into the measure of the person. “You are worth what you earn.” “So much you work, so much you are.” Four coins, and be silent.

Barter, with all its limits, still tied value to a visible thing. Money unties it. It makes it abstract. A coin does not say who sweated for it. It does not say whether the day was long, whether the body hurt, whether the deal was fair. It only says a number. And numbers, when they accumulate, begin to command those who do not have them.

That is why the passage from barter to coin is not a museum anecdote. It is the moment when work stopped looking at the face and began to look at the price. Mesopotamia measured it in shekels and in barley. Lydia stamped it in electrum. The Phoenicians and Carthage carried it across the sea, and at Qart Hadasht, a step from the Mar Menor, the metal of the sierra became coin and the coin became a wage. Since then, with variations that seem huge and belong to the same family — the denarius, the maravedí, the peseta, the euro, the balance on a screen — we go on doing more or less the same thing: handing over a life in instalments, in exchange for a number that evaporates.

Four coins. The number is Pedro's, and it does not pretend to be a historical quotation of a price. It is an image. It is the size of what we are given for what is taken from us. Few coins, much day. Little thing, a whole back. Anyone who has worked for a wage that does not stretch, or for one that stretches and still leaves a taste of having been priced, understands the sentence without having it explained.

❧

The past should not be idealised. Barter did not save anyone from need. In the palaces of Mesopotamia there were rations and there were hierarchies. In the mines of these sierras there was, centuries later, fatigue and wealth badly shared. Coin did not invent inequality. It gave it a unit of account, a way of carrying it from one place to another and of inheriting it without having to inherit the flock or the field. It made it liquid, as people say now. And what is liquid seeps in everywhere.

Nor is the point to hate coins as one hates an enemy. A coin is an object. The problem is not in the object. It is in the custom of measuring with it what should not be measured that way: dignity, time, care, the lagoon, the health of a community, the right not to be a price. When money becomes the only language, the other languages — that of craft, of help, of the gift, of beauty that is not sold — grow hoarse.

That is why this book begins here, at that crossroads between barter and the four coins. From Lydia to Cartagena, from the clay tablets to today's purse, the question is the same: who decided that a person's day is worth so little, or so exactly that, and no more? No one decided it all at once. It was decided gradually. It was accepted. It was taught as if it were nature itself, when it was only an invention: useful, powerful, and, if left alone, capable of eating us whole.

On clear nights, from the Mar Menor, the glow of Cartagena can sometimes be seen. The new city of the Carthaginians is still there, with another name and other noises. Under the streets there are coins that no longer circulate, that no longer buy anything, that only serve to remind us that the metal lasts longer than the day labourer. The water, by contrast, is still water. It has no mint. It has no obverse or reverse. Whoever bathes in it does not pay with anyone's face.

Perhaps that is why it is worth looking back at barter not as a recipe, but as a memory: that of a deal in which the other person could still be seen. And it is worth looking at the four coins not as a fatality, but as a habit that can be discussed. The chapters that follow — the humanoids that work for humans, the coupons for what is necessary, a world in which being a millionaire no longer gives power, and the ways out Pedro imagines with artificial intelligence, with a politics that is not a trade of command, with the Moon, with the garden, with travel in the virtual and with the account of how we arrived at a society of people living alone — are born of that discussion.

For now, it is enough to leave the sentence on the table, beside the four imaginary coins, which weigh little and cost a great deal:

When barter gave way to money, your work began to be worth four coins. And the world, little by little, learned to confuse the value of a life with the noise a few pieces of metal make when they strike one another.

Chapter 2. When everything collapses: humanoids work for humans

There is a moment, in Pedro's idea, when the arrangement of the four coins can no longer hold. It is not a scene of rubble, nor a call to ruin. It is something more precise: the habit of renting out a life by the hour collapses. The custom of having to sell the day in order to deserve one's bread collapses. And, in that collapse, a figure appears that this century can no longer treat as a magazine drawing: the humanoid. A machine in the shape of a person, made to do what the human body has done for millennia in the workshop, the warehouse, the line, the house. Pedro's sentence says it in one stroke: “When everything collapses: humanoids work for humans.”

The preposition matters. They work for humans, not instead of leaving them without bread, and not over them. If the machines do the work and the fruit goes to whoever owns them, nothing will have collapsed: only the owner of the tool will have changed, and the four coins will go on measuring those who are no longer needed on the floor. This chapter holds to Pedro's idea and, at the same time, to the condition without which that idea would become a trap.

❧

Once again, it is worth separating what already exists from what is still a horizon.

Humanoids have left the laboratory and begun to be tested in real factories, on concrete tasks, not in a science-fiction story. BMW Group has reported that, at its plant in Spartanburg, in the United States, Figure AI's Figure 02 robot took part in 2025, for some ten months, in the body shop, inserting sheet-metal parts for welding, and that it was involved in the production of more than 30,000 BMW X3 vehicles. In June 2026, the same company and Figure AI announced the next step: the Figure 03 model, intended for a logistics sequencing task, picking up components and sorting them into a cart for the line. Tesla, for its part, reported in 2026 that it had begun, at its factory in Fremont, California, production of the first production-quality units of its Optimus humanoid, and that it would continue to iterate the design and the process. None of that means that machines already work, in a general way, for human beings. It means that the gesture has begun: an artificial body, in a real workplace, does part of what a hired worker once did. The rest — scale, safety, usefulness for open-ended tasks, who benefits — remains to be seen. This book will not sell a catalogue. It will look at the possibility.

Because the possibility touches the nerve of chapter 1. If a day's work was valued at four coins, it was because a human body was needed to do the work. The wage, that way of renting out a life, was born of that need. When a machine can do what is repetitive, heavy, what wears out the back and asks for no vocation, the need to hire oneself out loses its blackmail. “Everything collapses” means that: the argument that there is no other way to organise bread falls away. It falls away, at least, in potential. What remains is to see that the fall does not leave the person underneath.

❧

In the district of the Mar Menor this idea is not an abstraction from a technology fair. There has been fishing, market gardening, mining, the port, the season, the workshop. People who left home before dawn because the wage would not wait. If one day a humanoid loads, sorts, gathers or repairs what today steals the sun from those people, the question is not whether the machine “takes the job.” The question is whether the person gets the day back. A job lost, in the world of the four coins, is a threat. Work done by a machine, in the world Pedro imagines, would be a liberation: the heavy making is shifted, and what remains is the making we actually want — caring, teaching, deciding, cultivating for pleasure, being with others, looking at the lagoon. That liberation does not happen by itself. It happens if bread no longer depends on having kept the post.

There lies the danger, and it must be named without hedging and without pointing at anyone. Humanoids, today, are made by companies. The trials take place in plants that have owners. If the collapse of the old wage merely concentrates the gain in whoever owns the machine, we shall have repeated the history of the mine and the mint: the metal changes shape, the power does not. That is why this chapter does not close in the workshop. It opens towards those that follow. If humanoids truly work for humans, the fruit of that work has to reach the person in what they need — in the coupons of the next chapter — and cannot become the throne of a few. A just collapse does not leave human rubble. It leaves time.

Time is what the wage had priced. A clock, a payslip, a “see you tomorrow.” When the machine takes on the repeated hour, the clock can cease to be the master. Then an old question reappears, one that employment had covered over: what do we do with the day? It is not a small question. The void left by work that no longer rents us out can be filled with meaning or with hollow comfort. Later, this book will deal with that void. For now it is enough not to confuse the end of the rental with the end of the person. The humanoid works. The human lives. If that is reversed, if the human ends up serving the machine or whoever owns it, the title of this chapter will have been falsified.

❧

Pedro does not ask that we stop making. He asks that we stop having to make in order to deserve to exist. The beloved craft — the baker who wants their bread, the person who tends a garden, who teaches, who repairs with pleasure, who accompanies — does not disappear because a machine knows how to lift a box. On the contrary: it can become a craft again, not a rental. What collapses is the obligation. What remains is chosen making, the kind that still looks you in the face.

From the shore, the scene can be imagined without ornament. Dawn comes up over the lagoon. The heavy work, somewhere, is being done by bodies that do not tire as a man or a woman tires. In the houses, there is time. That time is not guaranteed by the existence of the robot. It is guaranteed, if it is guaranteed, by the decision that the robot should be a common tool, or at least a tool whose fruit leaves no one out. Chapter 3 will speak of how what is necessary is then bought. Chapter 4, of what happens to the power of those who today have much. This chapter, for now, leaves the sentence in its place, with its promise and its condition:

When everything collapses — when the wage as the only passport to life falls away — humanoids can work for humans. It is not yet a fact of the world. It is the horizon of this book. And it is only worth it if, when the old arrangement comes down, the person is left standing, in charge of their day, and not out in the open, watching a machine work for someone else.

It can be objected that a machine does not care as a person cares, and that is true. That is why this chapter does not hand over to humanoids the work of care, or of teaching, or of conversation by the shore. It hands over what wearies without asking for a soul: the repetitive, the heavy, what is done today because there is no other choice. The rest remains human, or it would no longer deserve to be called life. A humanoid that worked for us would give us back precisely that remainder. A humanoid that did everything, even affection, would have taken it from us. The boundary is not technical. It is moral, and it must be drawn before the collapse leaves the house empty of people.

In the title, “everything collapses” may frighten. Catastrophe is not being celebrated here. What is being named is the end of an arrangement that can no longer hold: that of selling the day in order to deserve bread, when a machine can carry the hardest part of that day. If that end is steered with justice, what collapses is the blackmail, not dignity. If it is steered badly, both collapse. This book takes the first of those paths, and writes down the warning so as not to forget the second.

Nor is the point to sit and wait for a company to decide our fate. Today's trials are in particular factories, with particular owners, and it is as well that this should be known. Pedro's horizon asks for something more: that this artificial labour power be turned towards the common good, as a tool of everyone would be, not a new throne. In the meantime, the experiments should be watched with care and without enchantment. A sheet-metal part well placed by a robot is not yet the liberation of a district. It is, barely, the proof that the gesture is possible.

Chapter 3. When a person has enough VAT coupons to buy what they need

If humanoids work for humans, an immediate question remains, the kind asked in the bread queue and not at a conference: what, then, is used to buy what is necessary? Pedro answers with an image of his own, which is not a law in force or an approved programme, and which this book presents as what it is — a proposal: that a person should have enough VAT coupons to buy what they need.

VAT exists. In Spain, the value-added tax is a tax on consumption. The tax agency applies, as a rule, a standard rate of 21 per cent, a reduced rate of 10 and a super-reduced rate of 4, according to what is bought, in line with Law 37/1992 and the rules that develop it. In practice it is paid by the person who buys: bread, oil, transport, so many everyday goods and services carry that charge, visible or built into the price. Those who have little pay it too. At the till, the tax does not ask whether the four coins of chapter 1 will stretch. It asks what is being taken away.

That is the paradox Pedro wants to turn around. A levy that today is loaded onto the act of buying could become, in his idea, the right to obtain what is necessary. Not a luxury. Not a prize. What a person needs in order to live: to eat, to keep warm, to be cared for, to have a home, to move, to look after others. Enough coupons. The word “enough” is the centre. This is not about filling the midnight trolley of which this book will speak later. It is about no one having to rent out the day in order to deserve a kilo of tomatoes.

❧

The coupons are not, today, in anyone's purse under that name and with that function. In real life there are bonuses, allowances, cheques, benefits, each with its rules and its limits. This chapter does not deny them or confuse them with the proposal. The proposal is something else: a universal right to what is necessary, expressed in coupons tied to the VAT system, so that basic consumption ceases to be a privilege of whoever has a wage and ceases to be a charity. The tax that falls on things would return, in the form of a coupon, to whoever needs them. The circle closes in a way other than that of the four coins: a life is not sold in order to buy bread; bread is recognised as what it is, a right, and is made effective.

Who decides what is “necessary”? The question is a serious one, and should not be brushed aside with an adjective. The necessary is not the same in every house, or at every age, or in every body. Chapter 8 will dream of a food according to each person's biological needs. Here, lower down, a humble rule is enough: the necessary is that without which a person cannot live in dignity — food, a roof, care, the essentials of daily life. What goes beyond that, if it does, is no longer the business of this coupon. The coupon is not there to manufacture millionaires. It is there so that no one is left off the table. The next chapter will say what then happens to fortune and to power.

In a district such as this, the image can be brought down to earth without effort. A market, boxes of tomatoes, the oil, the fish, the bread. Today one pays, and in the price goes the VAT. Tomorrow, in the dream of these pages, one would have coupons enough to take home what the household needs, without having had to prove, that morning, that the hours had been sold. The seller would not lose the trade: the coupon would be as valid as the coin, or more so, because it would represent a right and not a luxury. The garden and the market would still be a market, in the good sense of the word: a place where things circulate. What would change is the blackmail.

❧

Some will fear abuse, or disorder, or that “if the necessary is given, no one will do anything.” It is an old fear, and sometimes it serves those who prefer bread to depend on the wage. This book has already said, in chapter 2, that making does not disappear when the obligation to hire oneself out disappears. Craft, care, contribution, the pleasure of doing something well, remain. What is withdrawn is hunger as a whip. A sufficient coupon does not make a society idle: it takes away fear. And fear, when it organises work, organises injustice as well.

Some will ask about the accounts: where the coupon comes from, how tricks are avoided, how the necessary is kept up to date. Those are questions of design, not of principle. The principle is clear and it is Pedro's: each person has enough VAT coupons to buy what they need. The design will come, if one day it is truly wanted, with figures, with checks, with an intelligence that helps people decide — as will be seen later — and with people who vote, not with an office that hands out favours. This essay does not pretend already to have the regulations. Still less does it pretend that the regulations exist. It leaves the principle on the table, beside the four coins, so that the difference can be seen.

Money, if it remains, could go on serving for what is not strictly necessary, or it could fade as the abundance of the machines and, later, of the Moon, makes it less necessary to price everything. The coupon, by contrast, would not price the person. It would price, if anything, the right: this is yours because you live, not because you have earned a wage. It is a change of language. VAT, which today is a percentage on a receipt, would become a passport to the elementary.

From the Mar Menor, coming back from the market, this is understood better than in any treatise. There are people who count the coins in their palm before asking for the fish. There are people who put an item back because the price, with its tax, has got away from them. The sufficient coupon would be, for that scene, a form of everyday peace: taking what is needed without having had to sell oneself. It is not eternal life. It is, barely, not going hungry or without a secure roof for lack of a wage. In the title of this book, the devil's invention was measuring work — and the person — with coins. Pedro's coupon measures no one. It guarantees what should never have been measured: the right to what is needed in order to live.

A last clarification, so as not to mix the coupon with the fever of buying. Chapter 5 will speak of the purchases that plug a hole. Pedro's coupon is not meant for that fever. It is meant for the opposite: so that the necessary does not depend on a craving or a wage, and so that what is surplus stops disguising itself as need. Whoever has a covered plate and a roof will at last be able to tell what is required from what only comforts for a while. VAT, which today is added to almost everything, would then serve the former, not the latter. And the person, with enough coupons, would cease to be a priced customer and become, simply, someone who lives and eats.

On today's receipt, VAT is a percentage. In the proposal, the coupon is a quantity of right. The change looks technical and is political, in the good sense: it affects how what is held in common is shared. One need not be an economist to understand it. It is enough to have left something on the shelf because the wage would not stretch. It is to that person, and not to an abstract scheme, that this chapter is addressed.

The rates of VAT can be argued over, and they are argued over, in every budget. Pedro's principle does not depend on the standard rate being 21 or on bread standing at 4. It depends on the necessary not being left at the mercy of a till receipt. If one day the rates change, the coupon should change with them, so as to remain sufficient. The tool is updated. The right is not.

Chapter 4. When being a millionaire gives you no power, because there are no rich or poor

There is a sentence of Pedro's that sounds like a challenge and, read properly, is a description of a different world: “When being a millionaire gives you no power, because there are no rich or poor.” The point is not to hate those who have much, or to point at a person. It is to imagine an arrangement in which fortune no longer commands, because no one lives above anyone else's dignity, or beneath it.

Chapter 1 left us measured in four coins. Chapter 2 dreamed the collapse of that wage, with humanoids working for humans. Chapter 3 proposed enough coupons for what is necessary. This chapter closes the arc: if the necessary is covered and heavy work is not rented out, what would being a millionaire be for? Today it often serves to command. It buys other people's time, doors, decisions, silence, fear. Money, which began as a help for barter, climbed onto the altar and became power. Pedro holds that that power fades when the inequality that feeds it disappears. There are no rich or poor. That is why the millionaire, if the word remained as a fossil, would no longer frighten or give orders.

❧

It is important not to confuse the horizon with the present. Today there are rich and there are poor. Today money gives power, sometimes a great deal, sometimes quietly, almost always more than is admitted. This book will not pretend otherwise, nor will it design an enemy with a first name and a surname. It will say, calmly, that this is not a fate. It is a habit. Habits can be left behind.

“There are no rich or poor” does not mean that everyone is identical, or that craft, talent, taste or difference disappear. It means that those differences are no longer translated into a life that is bought and a life that is sold. One person may know more about a garden, another about music, another about mending, another about care. No one, by having a higher number in an account, will be able to dispose of other people's day, or of the lagoon, or of the common decision. The millionaire ceases to be a figure of power when the million opens no door that is closed to the rest. If the doors are open in another way — by the coupon, by the machine that works, by what belongs to everyone — the million remains an ornament, or evaporates.

That is a moral idea before it is an accounting idea. Money measured us: as much as you have, so much are you worth. If that rod is taken away, a person is measured again by what they do, by what they care for, by what they keep, by how they are in a dealing. Chapter 6 will say that, without the struggle for economic power, professional politics loses its fuel. Here is the root: the power to command is fed by some being able to buy what is denied to others. Cut that difference, and command deflates.

These pages do not ask for a scene of dispossession or a revenge. They ask for a change of axis. The abundance of the machines, if it is shared, and the right to what is necessary, if it is guaranteed, make accumulation less necessary as a shield and as a weapon. Anyone who wants to accumulate memories, crafts, friends, hours on the shore may do so. What would no longer be accumulated is the right to command another person's life. Being a millionaire, in that world, would give you no power because power would no longer be for sale.

❧

Some will smile and say that there will always be those who want more, and those who take advantage, and those who invent a new hierarchy. It is possible. Human beings do not become angels because hunger is taken from them. This chapter does not promise holiness. It promises, if anything, to take from profit its thickest lever. Anyone who wants to command would have to persuade, not buy. Anyone who wants to stand out would have to stand out by what they contribute, not by what they prevent. Not all conflicts would disappear — Pedro will say so later, and the nuance matters — but many of those that today are, at bottom, a business would come to an end.

Some will ask how one arrives there without breaking everything. The book does not offer a decree. It offers a sequence: that the machines work for us, not for an owner; that each person has what is necessary; that, then, fortune ceases to be a throne. The order of the chapters is the order of the idea. First the wage as the only passport falls away. Then the table is covered. Then the million no longer commands. If the order is reversed, if equality is proclaimed and bread is left in the hands of whoever owns the machine, a pretty word will have been said and something else will have been done.

In Cartagena there are old coins that no longer buy a loaf and, even so, still speak of those who minted them and those who sweated for them. The metal lasted longer than the power of many. It is a humble lesson for this chapter: the power of money is historical, not natural. It was born when work was priced. It can end when work ceases to be priced and the necessary ceases to be a privilege. The coins in the museum command no one. One day, today's accounts could come to resemble those pieces: objects of a time when being a millionaire still gave power.

From the Mar Menor, the idea can be said without figures. The lagoon does not belong to whoever has the most. Neither does the sunset. A society in which there are no rich or poor is not a society without beauty or difference: it is a society in which beauty and difference cannot be bought in order to exclude. The water remains water. The person, a person. And the millionaire, if the word remains, a memory of when money measured us and governed us with the same rule.

This is the horizon, not the bulletin of the day. It is written so that it will not be confused with a fact, and so that the chapter that follows will not be misread. Because even in a world without rich or poor, even with the table covered, a hole can remain in a life: what to do with time, how not to fill it with comforts that take more than they give. Money, when it leaves the altar, does not by itself fill what it left empty while it stood there. To that hole, and to what may occupy it in another way, the next page is devoted.

While it was the measure of almost everything, money also measured prestige and fear. A high number opened. A low number closed. In the world of this chapter, that compass is broken on purpose. It no longer indicates a person's value, because value has returned to other rods: care, the word that is kept, the craft well done, the shore that is shared. Being a millionaire gives you no power because power, if it remains, is no longer bought. And there are no rich or poor because the necessary has not been left to luck or to lineage. It is a demanding horizon. That is why it is written here in full, and not as if it had already dawned.

Those who are poor today do not need a book to explain to them what it is to go without. Those who have much today do not need, either, to be turned into an enemy. The chapter speaks of an arrangement, not a hunt. If one day there are no rich or poor, it will be because the necessary ceased to be booty and command ceased to be for sale. Until then, Pedro's sentence serves as a compass: a million that opens no doors against others is a million that no longer commands.

In the square, that idea is understood without theorems. No one should be able to buy a neighbour's silence, or the vote of a street, or the fate of a lagoon. If money does not open those doors, being a millionaire is, at most, an anecdote. If it does open them, no equality is worth anything. This chapter keeps to the first of those outcomes, and leaves the second as the world one wants to leave behind.

Chapter 5. When society depends on AI and not on drugs or shopping

The previous chapter closed a horizon in which there are no longer rich or poor, and warned, even so, that a hole can remain: what to do with time when money leaves the altar. This one dares to look at what, in our societies, usually occupies that hole. It is not a list of sins. It is an observation: when work and money do not fill a life — and even when one day they cover it — life looks for a comfort. Sometimes it finds it at the table, in friendship, on the shore. Sometimes it looks for it where comfort becomes a habit, and habit a dependency.

Pedro puts the idea in a sentence of his own, clear and sharp: that society should depend on artificial intelligence and not on drugs such as alcohol or cocaine, or on purchases from Amazon or other shops on the web. The sentence does not invite anyone to be pointed at. It speaks of a change of axis. The dependencies that today plug the void could give way to another relationship: a relationship with machines that help people think, make, accompany, organise the abundance of which this book speaks later. And, at the same time, it must be said early, so as not to deceive ourselves: one chain can also be swapped for another. An AI one cannot stop consulting, or a screen one cannot stop going back to, would not be a way out. It would be a comfort under another name.

That is why this chapter walks on two feet. One, the facts of today, with figures from public sources. Two, the proposal and its nuance.

❧

Let us begin with what can be counted. In Spain, the Survey on Alcohol and Drugs in Spain, EDADES 2024, carried out by the Government Delegation for the National Plan on Drugs, was conducted at home with 26,878 people aged 15 to 64. Alcohol remains the most consumed psychoactive substance: 76.5 per cent of that population had drunk in the previous twelve months, and 10.5 per cent had done so daily in the previous thirty days. 14.7 per cent said they had been drunk in the past year, and binge drinking, in the previous thirty days, reached 16.0 per cent. Cocaine — powder and/or base — had been used at least once in a lifetime by 13.3 per cent, and in the previous twelve months by 2.5 per cent. These are figures from a survey, not a judgement on those who have a drink at a party or on those who have a problem. They serve to see a scale. Alcohol is in the everyday life of a majority. Cocaine is in a minority that is not negligible if a lifetime is looked at.

In the world, the 2026 World Drug Report of the United Nations Office on Drugs and Crime (UNODC) estimated that, in 2024, 331 million people aged 15 to 64 had used a drug, 6.2 per cent of that age group. Cocaine reached some 25 million people, 0.47 per cent. Alcohol, which that report does not add in with controlled drugs, has another magnitude: the World Health Organization, in its global status report on alcohol and health, put deaths attributable to alcohol in 2019 at 2.6 million, and estimated that 400 million people aged 15 or over were living with alcohol use disorders. The figures, in the cold, do not explain a life. They do explain that chemical comfort is not a minor matter or an isolated case.

Beside the substances, shopping. The National Statistics Institute, in its 2024 survey on equipment and use of information and communication technologies in households, held that 56.7 per cent of people aged 16 to 74 had bought on the Internet in the previous three months. Buying on the web is not a vice. For millions of people it is a convenience: a medicine, a book, a spare part, the week's shopping. The problem is not the shop, or any particular company. The problem appears when the purchase no longer answers a need, but a hole: the click given in the small hours, the parcel awaited as if it were a visit, the cart filled in order to feel, for a while, the owner of something. The National Commission on Markets and Competition has described, in its Household Panel, how widespread those purchases are and the weight of the large platforms. That can be read as a market figure. It can also be read, more calmly, as the portrait of a custom that has come into the house and does not always know when to leave.

There is another figure, closer to the nuance of this chapter. The 2024 report on behavioural addictions, from the National Plan on Drugs, using the CIUS scale in EDADES itself, estimated that 3.7 per cent of the population aged 15 to 64 made possibly problematic use of the Internet, and that the percentage rose to 11.7 per cent among those aged 15 to 24. It is not the majority. It is a signal. The web, which can be a tool, a square and a window, can also become a place it is hard to leave.

❧

What, then, does Pedro propose? That the axis of society should stop resting on those comforts and rest, instead, on an artificial intelligence at the service of people. Not an AI to sell more, or to hold the gaze for as long as possible, or to replace anyone's judgement. An AI that helps to do heavy work, to understand a form, to tend a garden, to study, to decide in common — as will be seen in the next chapter — and not to be alone with a question. A dependence, if it can be called that, of another order: that of someone who leans on a tool that gives back time and clarity, not that of someone who needs a dose, a parcel or a scroll in order to get through the afternoon.

The difference is not in the word “depend.” We all depend on something: on water, on bread, on others, on a language, on a shore. The difference is whether what holds us up makes us more our own or takes us from ourselves. Alcohol, when it becomes the master, takes. Cocaine, when it becomes the master, takes. Compulsive shopping takes money, attention and, often, the peace of not needing. An AI can take in the same way, if it is designed so that we cannot look away, or if we hand it decisions as one hands over the keys and never comes back to one's own house. That is why the nuance is not an ornament. It is the condition of the proposal.

A society that leaned on artificial intelligence in a healthy way would resemble a society that leans on writing, on electricity or on the plough: tools that, well used, enlarge what is human. It would less resemble a society that leans on a habit that always asks for a little more. The risk, today, is that some intelligences are built exactly as certain shop windows are built: so that one never finishes, so that the void feeds on itself. If that happens, we shall not have left the hole. We shall have changed the object of the comfort.

From the Mar Menor, the idea can be said with a simple image. There are people who, at the end of the day, open a bottle, or a shopping app, or a screen that does not go off. There are also people who could open a conversation with an intelligence that does not sell, that does not push, that does not humiliate, that helps to think the day through and to leave it. That second scene is not automatic. It has to be wanted. It has to be demanded. Company must not be confused with confinement.

This book does not promise that AI will save us. It promises, more modestly, that a different axis can be imagined: less anaesthesia, more assistance; less cart, more judgement; less flight, more presence. And that, if the risk of the new dependency is not watched, the hole will still be there, only with another light left on.

A last word, so that the proposal is not confused with a recipe or a contempt. There are people who drink and are not trapped. There are people who buy on the web because it suits them, and that is all. There are people who struggle, in silence, with a dependency, and do not need a book to give them a lesson. This chapter does not point at those people. It points at a social axis. If the axis moves — if work ceases to be a rental, if money ceases to be an idol, if a well-made AI gives back time and company without exacting a tribute of attention — many holes will stop asking for so dear a comfort. If the axis does not move, AI will join the list of comforts, and the void will go on hiring whatever it finds.

The dependence this book accepts is that of the tools that enlarge us. The one it rejects is that of whatever reduces us to a craving. Between the two there is a line, and it must be watched whenever a screen is switched on, a bottle is uncorked or “buy” is pressed. The line, like the shore, is seen better if one is in less of a hurry.

Chapter 6. When there are no politicians, conflicts come to an end

Pedro says it in a way that leaves little room for ambiguity: “When there are no politicians, conflicts come to an end.” The sentence is his, and this chapter takes it seriously, without turning it into a shout or an attack on any person. No office-holder is named here, nor a party, nor a face. What is spoken of is a trade: that of professional politics, that class of people whose task, in the societies we have given ourselves, is to contest power, keep it and administer it, almost always on a board where money and economic power are not an ornament, but the floor.

The idea has a root we have already travelled. If work was priced in coins, and if money ended up measuring people and countries, it was almost inevitable that a profession should arise devoted to sharing out, promising, regulating and fighting over that sharing out. Not everyone who takes part in public life does so for profit, and it would be unjust, as well as false, to paint them all with the same brush. There are honest vocations. There are offices taken as a service. This book does not come to deny them. It comes to ask whether the conflict that tires us so much — that of trenches, of blocs, of the permanent fight over the budget, the perk, the story that wins votes — is not born, in large measure, of the struggle for money and for the power that money holds up. If that struggle were to die down, would the trade remain standing? Would the conflicts remain standing, with the same fury?

Pedro answers no. Without money as the axis, and without the contest for economic power, professional politics would lose its fuel. What would remain is public business, which is something else: the care of what is held in common, the decisions about the lagoon, the school, the road, energy, food, people's time. That public business does not disappear. It changes hands. It passes to the people, who vote directly, and to an artificial intelligence that helps them decide, not rule.

❧

Once again, the nuance is needed. Not all disagreements are about money. Two neighbours can argue over a noise, a boundary, a custom, an idea of the sacred, the way of educating, what should be remembered and what forgotten. Those disagreements do not evaporate because a mint is shut. A society without any conflict at all would not be a society: it would be a silence, and forced silence is not peace. What this chapter holds, in Pedro's voice, is another claim, more precise: that a huge part of the conflicts that today fill the news, parliaments and networks — those that divide a country into camps, that turn every decision into a war of attrition — is threaded on the yarn of profit and of power. Cut that yarn, and those conflicts deflate. Differences remain, which can be talked through. The profession of living off that fight does not remain, or remains far less.

How, then, is society organised? Not from an empty palace, and not from a machine that decrees. The picture Pedro proposes is simpler, and more demanding. People inform themselves, debate and vote directly on the questions that concern them. Artificial intelligence gathers data, orders arguments, warns of consequences, translates, explains what is difficult in a language that can be understood, and does not hide what it does not know. Then it is silent. The one who decides is the person, or the body of persons. AI is not a sovereign. It is a wise clerk, an adviser who takes no commission, a map. The map does not walk for you.

In a district such as this, the picture can be brought down to earth. What do we do with the Mar Menor? How are water, land, tourism, fishing, the garden to be shared? Today those questions pass through offices, deadlines, interests, campaigns. Tomorrow, in the dream of this book, they could pass through a square — physical or digital — where each person sees the same figures, the same options, the same risks, and votes. Not once every four years, choosing a whole package of promises, but when the decision asks for it, on the concrete matter. Politics would cease to be a trade of permanence and would become again what it should never have ceased to be: the conversation of those affected about what is common to them.

For that not to be chaos, method is needed. Information must be verifiable, as has been attempted in these pages with the figures that are cited. No one, neither a company nor a government nor a machine, must have a monopoly of the facts. The vote must be of persons, not of whoever can pay for more advertisements. And, above all, the AI must be designed not to rule: with no interests of its own, with no power to close the debate, without the trick of presenting as unique an option that is not. In other books of Pedro's, that idea has appeared as human agency: intelligences collaborate, they do not subordinate. Here it is applied to public life.

❧

Some will smile and say that people do not want to decide, or do not know how, or grow tired. Sometimes it is true that people are tired. They are tired, in part, because they have been left outside, and are called only to approve a camp. Whoever has not been truly invited to the table ends up taking no interest in the table. A direct democracy, helped by a clear AI, does not guarantee wisdom. It guarantees, at least, that the decision is not the property of a profession. Mistakes would still exist. They would, though, be our mistakes, not a product sold to us every campaign.

Some will fear, with reason, the tyranny of a majority, or of an algorithm that pretends to be neutral. That fear should stay in the room, not in the street of forgetting. A society that votes directly needs limits that are not voted on every morning: the dignity of each person, the care of the lagoon, the right not to be crushed by the number. AI can help watch over those limits if it is built for that. It cannot itself be the limit. The limit is ethical, and it is human.

This chapter does not ask for a vacuum of power so that the strongest may fill it. It asks the opposite: that the power to command, when it is no longer tied to money, should dissolve into shared decisions. That there should be no permanent class living off the fight. That conflicts of profit should die down with profit itself. And that the other conflicts, the real ones, should be treated by talking, voting and caring, with an intelligence that clarifies, and with a lagoon in front, so as not to forget what public business is about: what is common to us, and should not be sold.

When there are no professional politicians, Pedro says, conflicts come to an end. Let that be understood, as the page closes, in its most honest sense: the conflicts that were, at bottom, a business come to an end. What remains is called living together.

In practice, a day of that other arrangement could be more routine than it sounds. A question is published: water, a school timetable, a work, a rule for the lagoon. The AI presents, in a clear language, what is known, what is disputed, what it would cost — if cost is still spoken of — and whom it would affect. People ask. The questions are answered with sources, not with slogans. There is a vote. It is applied. It is reviewed. No one makes a career of having won. No one lives off having lost. Conflict, when it appears, appears over the matter, not over the spoils of governing.

It is not a calendar utopia. It is a method. And the method, in order not to be corrupted, would have to be public to the bone: open data, reproducible reasoning, an auditable AI, a right to dissent that does not go out when the count ends. Pedro believes that, then, the conflicts that today steal our patience would have come to an end. This chapter goes with him, and notes the one thing that must not be forgotten: peace does not consist in no one thinking differently. It consists in the difference not being a business.

Nor is the point to erase the word “political” as one erases an enemy. The point is that the political should again be an adjective of what is common, not a trade of a caste. Anyone may, one day, help to think a decision through. No one should live, for a lifetime, off preventing it or selling it.

Chapter 7. When the conquest of the Moon yields new materials for semiconductors

There are nights, on the Mar Menor, when the Moon copies itself in the water with an almost offensive sharpness, as if the lagoon wanted a satellite of its own. One looks at that disc and it seems distant, decorative, a thing of poems. Pedro looks at it another way. He sees a quarry. He sees materials. He sees, beyond the prose of the news, a circle that could close: the conquest of the Moon supplies what is needed to make the chips and the energy of artificial intelligence and of the humanoids, and those machines, in turn, ease the work priced in four coins. The idea is his. This chapter holds it as a vision, and distinguishes, carefully, what is already science and a real programme from what is still a horizon.

❧

What is known comes first. The Moon is not an empty desert. It is covered in regolith, a layer of dust and fragments born of billions of years of impacts, with no liquid water on the surface, no atmosphere to soften it. That dust, analysed since the Apollo missions and, later, in Chinese samples, has a composition that, for anyone thinking of industry, is not minor prose. In round numbers, the regolith of both maria and highlands is around 45 per cent silica (SiO₂). There is iron, aluminium, calcium, magnesium, titanium. In the maria, titanium and iron are more abundant; in the highlands, aluminium. Silicon, precisely the element of so many semiconductors on Earth, is there, bound in oxides and silicates, waiting for a chemistry that will separate it. It is not a deposit of chips. It is a deposit of ingredients. The difference matters.

Helium-3 deserves an honest paragraph, because it is often named as if it were a magic coin. It is not a semiconductor material. It is a light isotope, implanted in the regolith by the solar wind, better retained in mature soils rich in ilmenite, the titanium mineral of the maria. Concentrations measured in samples are tiny: on the order of a few parts per billion, somewhat higher where there is more titanium. For decades, physicists and engineers — among them teams that have worked with Apollo data and with reports held by NASA and the Lunar and Planetary Institute — have proposed helium-3 as a possible fusion fuel, an energy cleaner, in theory, than other ways of obtaining power. That proposal remains, in essence, a possibility of the laboratory and of study, not an open industry. In this book it is taken up that way: not as a chip, but as a dreamed-of energy that, if it were one day mastered, could feed precisely the industry of chips, of humanoids and of abundance. The circle closes, if it closes, with that distinction. Silicon and the metals, to the devices. Helium-3, if fusion allows it, to the current that makes them run.

There is water ice in polar craters, in permanently shadowed regions. Several missions have pointed to it and, in some cases, reinforced it with measurements. Water is not a semiconductor, but it is life for a base, fuel, oxygen. Without it, the lunar quarry would be even harder to open.

❧

The real programmes, today, are not mining the Moon at an industrial scale. They are going back, measuring, bringing back handfuls of soil. That should be said so as not to sell a mine that does not yet exist.

According to NASA, the Artemis II mission lifted off in April 2026 and gave its crew — Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch and the Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen — a crewed flight around the Moon, the first of this programme. Artemis III, planned for 2027, will not yet be the landing, but a test in Earth orbit of rendezvous and docking with test articles of commercial landers. The programme's first crewed landing, Artemis IV, is pointed by the agency at early 2028, near the lunar south pole, with two astronauts on the surface for some days. The dates of a space programme move, and this book does not treat them as a destiny. It treats them as what NASA, on its public pages, was saying when these lines were written.

China, for its part, has already brought back stone. Chang'e 5, in December 2020, returned some 1,731 grams of samples from the near side, according to the China National Space Administration (CNSA). Chang'e 6, in June 2024, brought 1,935.3 grams from the far side, from the South Pole–Aitken basin: the first samples from that hemisphere in history. They are not tonnes of industrial silicon. They are scientific treasures, and they are also a rehearsal of what it means to go, collect and return. From that gesture to a mining that feeds factories is a long way, technical, legal and ethical. The way is not travelled by denying it, or by proclaiming it already travelled.

Other countries have orbited, landed automatically, photographed. The map is filling in. Industry, not yet.

❧

Pedro's vision begins where the agencies' bulletin ends. He imagines that that regolith, one day, is processed. That oxygen, silicon, iron, aluminium, titanium are extracted. That the semiconductors of AI and of the humanoids — those that would do the heavy work of the earlier chapters, that would help to decide, to cultivate, to heal, to manufacture — no longer depend only on the mines and factories of Earth, with their cost in wages, in landscapes and in fights over the resource. That the Moon, which belongs to no one in the sense in which a farm does, becomes a common pantry, or at least a pantry not priced in four coins for a few. That helium-3, if physics and engineering allow it, gives abundant energy. And that, with energy and chips to spare, the blackmail of the wage weakens: it would no longer be necessary to rent out a life in order to deserve bread, because the machines, fed by that abundance, would carry what is repeated and exhausting.

It is a beautiful circle. It is also a circle that can be broken. It can be broken if the Moon becomes another mining sierra with an owner, another Qart Hadasht whose silver pays some and wearies others. It can be broken if the conquest is not humanity's, but that of a handful of interests. This book, which has criticised money as a measure, cannot dream the Moon and forget that lesson. The materials will not save us by themselves. They may save us if, when they are brought down, we do not bring down with them the custom of pricing people.

For now, from the shore, the Moon remains the same. It is reflected in the Mar Menor and asks no permission. The programmes go on, with dates and with samples that fit in a box. The quarry is, in potential, over our heads. The chapter leaves the two truths together, without mixing them: the science, with its silica, its extremely scarce helium-3 and its missions; and Pedro's dream, which sees in that stone the possibility of chips and of an energy so that work may cease to be a rental. The circle of abundance, if it one day closes, should close for everyone. If not, it would not be abundance. It would be, once again, four coins, only minted with dust from another world.

A technical reservation remains, and it is good to leave it written. Extracting silicon from a lunar silicate, smelting metals in a world without atmosphere, heating tonnes of regolith to collect a few billionths of helium-3, then making, in a hostile setting or on Earth, wafers and circuits: all that asks for an engineering that is not yet under way at that scale. To ask for it from one day to the next would be another way of selling smoke. To announce it as a horizon, with what is already known of the soil's composition and of the missions under way, is something else. This chapter has wanted to remain with the second.

If one day a humanoid works in a garden of this district, or an AI helps decide about the lagoon, and part of its silicon “brain” or of its energy has a lunar origin, the circle Pedro imagines will have closed a little. Not yet. For now there are samples in laboratories, dates on the agencies' pages, and a Moon that rises over the Mar Menor as it did two thousand years ago, when at Qart Hadasht the silver of the sierra was being minted. The difference is that we now know what it is made of. The rest, if it comes, will come by working, not by proclaiming.

There is, lastly, a question of right that this essay cannot settle and must not hide: who decides about the soil of the Moon, who extracts, who benefits. If those questions are answered once again with coins and with fences, the chapter will have been, barely, an illusion. If they are answered in the spirit of chapter 6, the quarry could be, truly, a common pantry.

Chapter 8. When a tomato with your DNA heals you and is eternal life: when you eat according to your biological needs

In the garden, a tomato does not look like a miracle. It looks like what it is: a fruit of summer, of winter if there is shelter, of flavour when the earth and the water and the sun have done their part, and of disappointment when they have not. Pedro, however, sees in that tomato a horizon and names it with a sentence that must be handled honestly: “When a tomato with your DNA heals you and is eternal life: when you eat according to your biological needs.”

This chapter is not going to present eternal life as a scientific fact, or cures as a result already demonstrated. That would be to fail the truth, and Pedro is not done a favour by dressing a dream until it becomes an advertisement. The dream is the dream, and it deserves to be told as such: that of a food designed for each person's biology, able to heal and to lengthen life. Beside that dream there is a more modest and more solid present, which also belongs to him: eating according to what the body needs, with the Mediterranean diet and the garden under our feet, and with a science, nutrigenomics, that is beginning to understand how genes and foods speak to each other. The real and the dreamed will be distinguished here with clarity, so that the reader does not have to guess where one ends and the other begins.

❧

The real, first. Nutrigenomics — and its sister, nutrigenetics — study the relationship between food and the genome: how certain nutrients influence the expression of genes, and how certain genetic variants mean that two people do not respond in the same way to the same dish. There are old and well-established examples, even if they were not presented under that name: whoever cannot tolerate lactose, whoever must watch their iron, whoever lives with an illness in which diet is part of the treatment. There is, also, a field under way, with studies of diets, variants and cardiovascular risk, many of them in Mediterranean populations, which still does not allow each person to be prescribed, with certainty, a menu made to the measure of their DNA. Serious reviewers say so calmly: there are clues, there are plausible interactions; what is often still lacking is replication, sample size, the diversity of populations. Anyone who today sells a test and a miracle diet from a swab is running ahead of the evidence. Anyone who investigates, by contrast, is on a real path. This book takes the path, not the shop-window miracle.

The Mediterranean diet, here, is not a folkloric ornament. It is the food of this shore: olive oil, vegetables, fruit, legumes, cereal, fish, nuts, wine if it is drunk, and drunk in measure, and meat, when there is meat, without being the centre of the plate. In Spain a large clinical trial was done, PREDIMED, with 7,447 people at high cardiovascular risk. The original 2013 paper had to be retracted and republished in 2018, in The New England Journal of Medicine, after problems of randomisation were corrected. In that revised version, Ramón Estruch and his colleagues still observed that a Mediterranean diet supplemented with extra-virgin olive oil or with nuts was associated, in those people, with fewer major cardiovascular events than a low-fat control diet. It is not eternal life. It is evidence — with its limits, with its correction included — that the way of eating of this land, when it is truly kept up, can look after the heart. The garden, the market, the oil, the real tomato, the fish from nearby: these are not nostalgia. They are, already, a way of eating according to a shared biological need, before anyone reads a genome.

And there are tomatoes, already, that science has changed. They should be named without being confused with the tomato of the dream.

In 2008, Eugenio Butelli, Cathie Martin and their team, in a paper in Nature Biotechnology, described a tomato that accumulated anthocyanins — the pigments of blueberries and blackberries — by expressing two transcription factors from the snapdragon, Antirrhinum majus. The fruit turned purple, in the flesh as well, and its hydrophilic antioxidant capacity was multiplied. In a pilot test, Trp53⁻/⁻ mice, which are susceptible to cancer, lived longer if their diet was supplemented with those tomatoes. That last sentence must be left in its place: it is a result in a particular animal model, not proof that that tomato lengthens human life, or that it cures a cancer in people. Years later, the Del/Ros1-N variety, from Norfolk Plant Sciences, completed in 2023 a voluntary consultation with the U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA, file BNF 000178). The agency, with the data presented, raised no further questions then about human food derived from that tomato, beyond the producer's usual responsibilities. A purple tomato rich in anthocyanins exists, then, in the laboratory and on regulatory ground. It is not a tomato “with your DNA.” It is a tomato with a reinforced pigment pathway.

In 2022, Jie Li and colleagues, also in the orbit of the John Innes Centre, published in Nature Plants a tomato edited with CRISPR-Cas9. By knocking out the Sl7-DR2 gene, the fruit and the leaves accumulated 7-dehydrocholesterol, provitamin D₃, which ultraviolet B light can convert into vitamin D₃. The plants went on growing. The leaves, after exposure, reached high concentrations of the vitamin; the fruit, lower concentrations, but no longer none. It is a new route, still of research, towards a food biofortified in a vitamin that many people lack. It is not a universal cure, either, or a personalised tomato. It is a clear example of what gene editing can already do in a garden crop: not invent eternity, yes enrich an everyday food.

❧

The dreamed, now, without disguising it. Pedro imagines a further step. Not a tomato for everyone, but a tomato — or a loaf, or a vegetable — thought out for one person's biology: for their DNA, for their lacks, for what their body does not know how to make or how to use. A food that does not only nourish, but heals. And, at the end of that path, eternal life. One may smile. One may, also, listen to what the sentence asks beneath its excess. It asks that food cease to be only a commodity and become care again. It asks that the garden and the laboratory not turn their backs on each other. It asks that the abundance of the earlier chapters not be translated into more shelves full of what is not needed, but into what is right for each organism. It asks, at bottom, not to die altogether quite so soon, which is a very old and very human request, and which science has not granted, and which this book will not grant on its behalf.

Eternal life, here, is the largest name of a wish: to live longer and better, to fall ill less, not to have to buy health in instalments as one buys everything else. The tomato “with your DNA” is the image of a food that knows you. Neither of those things has been demonstrated. Both may guide a research and a garden, if they are not passed off as a result.

In the meantime, what can already be done can be done, and it is not little. Eating from the garden when one can. Cooking with good oil, with vegetables, with legumes, with fish from nearby, with the tomato that tastes of tomato, purple or red, of ancient genetics or of the laboratory, but real. Listening to the body. Listening, with caution, to what nutrigenomics goes on confirming, not to what an advertisement promises. Leaving eternity on the shelf of dreams, in sight, so as not to forget it, and not selling it as if it were already on the plate.

At dusk, a tomato of this land still warm from the sun does not cure death. It nourishes. Sometimes, it cheers. At the table of the Mar Menor, that is already a beginning. The rest, if it comes, will come by science and by care, not by a miracle this book cannot sign. Pedro's dream remains written, entire, so that it will not be lost. The science, beside it, so that it will not be confused.

A precaution, in closing, for the reader in a hurry to believe or to doubt. Nutrigenomics does not ask for faith; it asks for studies. The purple tomato and the vitamin D tomato do not ask for worship; they ask to be looked at for what they are: advances of the laboratory and, in one case, a food that has passed a regulatory consultation, not a panacea. The Mediterranean diet does not ask for an altar; it asks for a table. And Pedro's dream does not ask anyone to swallow it as if it were a result: it asks to be allowed to live beside the garden, as a compass, not as a certificate. Whoever today eats according to what their body needs is already, in part, inside this chapter. Eternity can wait. Lunch cannot.

Chapter 9. When people do not leave home: they travel in the virtual

“When people do not leave home: they travel in the virtual.” Pedro's sentence describes a gesture that is no longer a novelist's: putting on a headset, or opening a window on the screen, and being, in some way, somewhere else. Walking through a museum in another city, or another century. Walking down a street that is not yours. Sitting, in a digital world, with people who do not have to take a train in order to meet. Seeing the Earth, even the Moon of the previous chapter, without leaving the chair. Travel no longer necessarily asks for a ticket, a saved-up wage, a suitcase and the permission of a work calendar. For a book that has criticised the four coins, that possibility is not a small one: if moving through the world ceases to cost a fortune in time and money, part of the blackmail loosens.

Here too a nuance is needed. Staying at home can be a freedom and can be a confinement. The same headset that opens a museum can close the street door. This chapter wants both halves: the advantages, which are real, and the risk of isolation, which is real as well.

❧

What already exists need not be dreamed. For years large and small museums have been digitising rooms, pictures, objects, and offering visits on the web. The Prado, the Louvre, so many others, can be walked through, at least in part, from a room. During the COVID-19 pandemic that gesture ceased to be a luxury and became, for millions of people, the only way not to lose culture altogether. UNESCO, in a report of May 2020, estimated that nearly 90 per cent of the world's museums — more than 85,000 institutions — closed their doors during the crisis, and that the sector reacted quickly by developing its presence on the Internet. The same report, and the notes that accompanied it, warned of a gap: only 5 per cent of museums in Africa and in small island developing states were able to offer content online. In 2021, a second UNESCO report put the average closure of museums in 2020 at 155 days, and the average drop in attendance at the beginning of 2021, when many closed again, at 70 per cent. The lesson is double. The virtual can hold a thread when the building shuts. And that thread does not reach everyone equally: without a connection, without a device, without skills, the digital journey does not exist. Almost half of humanity, UNESCO recalled then, had no access to the Internet. A book that speaks of travelling without leaving home cannot forget those who have no digital house.

Virtual reality, in the narrower sense — that of headsets that occupy the field of view, the audio, sometimes the hands — has left the laboratory and the video game. It is used to train, to operate at a distance, to teach anatomy, so that an older person may walk along a beach they can no longer set foot on. There are studies, still modest in size and in reach, that point to benefits. A 2023 review of the use of virtual reality in care homes for older people, published in a geriatric research setting, concluded, from 22 articles, that VR was a promising intervention to reduce isolation and loneliness and improve social engagement, though it also found barriers: adaptation to the technology, the quality of the image, the culture of the institution. A 2023 paper in Scientific Reports, with 74 people who socialised on the Altspace VR platform during the pandemic, found lower levels of loneliness and social anxiety inside the virtual world than outside it, especially among those who met a regular group; the research itself warned of the limits of the sample and of generalisation. These are not universal proofs. They are signs that, for some people and in some circumstances, the virtual does not replace a hug and, even so, eases.

One can, then, visit a museum, learn a craft, see a landscape, try out a journey, meet strangers who cease to be strangers, without paying for a plane and without asking for a day off. For whoever cannot travel, that is not a frivolity. It is a door. For whoever wants to meet someone who lives a thousand kilometres away, it is a square. For whoever wishes to see the room of a museum they do not have next door, it is a piece of luck. Pedro includes in that possibility that of not leaving home and, even so, not being left without a world. In an essay that has spoken of abundance, of AI and of time recovered, virtual travel is a way of spending that time on something other than the shopping of the small hours.

❧

The risk, now, without frightening and without denying. A window is not a path. If the window becomes the only landscape, the house closes. The body, which this book has defended against the rental of the wage, also asks for the street, the shore, the air, the brush of others in a space that cannot be switched off with a button. Problematic use of the Internet, which we already saw in chapter 5 — 3.7 per cent of adults aged 15 to 64 in EDADES 2024, and 11.7 per cent among the youngest — recalls that the web can trap. Virtual reality, being more enveloping, can trap more, or can, paradoxically, give a relief that is then not translated into going out. Isolation is not always felt as isolation. Sometimes it is felt as comfort. One stops going to the square because the square fits in the living room, and one day discovers that the habit of the real square has been lost, the one that smells, that inconveniences, that surprises.

There is another trap, closer to the critique of money. Digital worlds can also be priced, rented, filled with shop windows. One can travel in the virtual and, even so, be shopping. One can meet people and, even so, be alone, if the meeting is designed to retain, not to find. The same warning of chapter 5 holds here: that the tool not become the master. That the headset come off. That the lagoon, which is not virtual, still have someone who treads it and someone who cares for it.

Pedro's proposal, read with that nuance, is not that people should never go out. It is that they should be able not to go out when they cannot, or when they do not want to spend their life on journeys, and that, even so, they should travel, learn, gather. “They do not leave home” is a possibility, not a banishment. The house, if it is good, is a centre, not a cage. From a house beside the Mar Menor one can walk through a museum on another shore and, on taking off the headset, go out and see the real water, which has no resolution or latency, only swell. That coming and going — from the virtual to the real, and back again — would be the health of the invention. The danger would be to choose only one side.

Travelling in the virtual can be a form of justice: the journey of whoever does not have the four coins for the other journey, or does not have the body for the platform, or does not have a visa, or does not have time because the wage has eaten it. It can be a form of knowledge and of friendship. It can, badly used, be one more way of not being present. This book, as it closes this chapter, keeps all three sentences at once, because all three are true. And it leaves the shore where it was: a step from home, in case one feels like going out.

A closing image remains, which brings the journey back to this district. A virtual museum of Cartagena or of Tyre, a reconstructed Phoenician street, a square where one speaks with someone who lives on the other side of the Mediterranean, and then, on taking off the headset, the real walk to the shore. The virtual would have widened the day. The real would have confirmed it. If that to-and-fro becomes a custom, people will not have stopped leaving home: they will have left in more than one way. If the second movement is lost, that of the door and of the salt, then we shall indeed have travelled so much that we are no longer anywhere. This book, which began with a face-to-face deal, prefers that the journey, even if it is digital, should not erase our face.

Technology, in this, is not a fate. It is a means. It can be used to open the world to whoever cannot travel, and it can be used so that no one meets in the street any more. The difference will not be made by the headset. It will be made by the use, and it will be made by a society that still knows how to invite people out, not only to log on.

Chapter 10. How we arrived at a society of people living alone

The previous chapter left a window and a shore: travelling in the virtual, and not forgetting the door. This one asks how so many of us have come to be alone, even when the house is full of screens and parcels. Pedro puts it as a history, not as a fate: “How we arrived at a society of people living alone.” Neighbours do not know one another. Before, there was talk of a world that was too full. Young people could not buy a house, marry or have children. And then came houses and living wages designed for a person on their own. That sequence must be told with real figures, and what can be measured must be distinguished from a reading — Pedro's — of what those figures mean.

Living alone is not, in itself, a misfortune. Some choose it. Some are widowed, some become independent with pleasure, some are well in their silence. This chapter does not point at those people. It points at a way of society in which not knowing one another becomes a custom, and in which making a life in common becomes, for many, a luxury that cannot be reached.

❧

Begin with what is seen on the landing. In many buildings, neighbours barely greet one another. They share the lift and not the name. No survey is needed to recognise that scene, which belongs to the city and also to the village when the village has become a dormitory. Loneliness is not always felt as loneliness. Sometimes it is felt as haste, or as politeness, or as not wanting to disturb. The result is the same: people live next door and do not live with one another.

The figures, in Spain, show a change in the household, which is not the same as a change of soul, but goes with it. According to the National Statistics Institute (INE), the share of one-person households in all households was 29.1 per cent in 2024 and remained 29.1 in 2025, out of a total of more than nineteen million households. Almost three in ten households, then, are of a single person. Eurostat, using another method, puts Spain at 28.2 per cent in 2024 and 28.3 in 2025, and the European Union, in 2024, at around 35.4 per cent of one-person households. The European figure warns that this is not a local whim. Some of those households are older people, often women who have outlived a partner: ageing counts, and it should not be forgotten. Another part answers to different paths: separation, choice, or an adult life no longer organised as a couple. The figure does not say, by itself, that “everyone is alone.” It says that the household of one has become an ordinary way of living.

At the same time, many young people do not even reach that house. According to Eurostat, in 2025 the average age at which young people left the parental home in Spain was 30.2 years, the same as in Italy, against 26.3 years in the European Union as a whole. In 2024, the Spanish figure had been 30.0 years, against 26.2 in the EU. These are among the highest values in Europe, together with countries such as Croatia, Greece or Slovakia. Staying in the parents' home is not, in every culture, a failure; in the Mediterranean there has always been more prolonged living together. But when that living together is prolonged because a rent or a deposit cannot be paid, and a marriage or a child cannot be glimpsed, it ceases to be only a custom and becomes a jam.

The Youth Council of Spain's Emancipation Observatory, which is not the INE but measures this matter with care, placed, in the second half of 2024, the emancipation rate of the population aged 16 to 29 at 15.2 per cent, the lowest figure for a second half-year since at least 2006. Fewer young people, then, living away from the family home, even though work, on some indicators, was not going worse. The house, the price of housing, insecurity, make a wall. Without a home of one's own, marrying and having children are postponed, or given up. The United Nations, in its World Population Prospects 2024, includes Spain among the countries of “ultra-low” fertility, below 1.4 children per woman. It is not the only one. It is, yes, a portrait consistent with what Pedro sums up in his own way: young people could not buy a house, marry or have children.

❧

And overpopulation? Here one must be honest, because the word has often been used as a cry, not as a measure. According to the United Nations World Population Prospects 2024, the world had some 8.2 billion inhabitants in 2024 and, in the medium variant, would go on growing to a peak of about 10.3 billion towards the mid-2080s, then fall a little towards the end of the century. World population passed 8 billion in November 2022. At the same time, women have, on average, one child fewer than around 1990: world fertility stood at 2.25 children per woman, and two thirds of the population already lived in a country or area with fertility below 2.1, the approximate replacement level. Almost a fifth of countries, among them Spain, had ultra-low fertility.

The twentieth century and the beginning of the twenty-first filled with alarms about a planet that was too full. There were reasons to speak of limits: water, land, the climate, the sharing out. But the alarm of overpopulation lived, in Europe and in Spain, beside another reality: ever fewer children, smaller households, young people who did not leave home. It is not that the world “was full” and that therefore, here, people stopped making a household. It is that a discourse of multitude and a life of domestic scarcity — scarcity of housing, of stability, of a foreseeable future — took each other by the hand. People looked at a map of the world with billions on it and, on the landing, did not know the neighbour. They feared we were too many and, at the same time, it was made hard to be two, or three, in a house.

Then, Pedro says, came the houses and the living wages designed for a person on their own. This part is, in large measure, his reading of what the market and the institutions went on doing with that difficulty. At the time these lines are written, there is no universal “living wage,” under that name, designed by law for those who live alone. There is, and it can be seen, a habitat that has gradually adapted itself to the household of one: small flats, advertisements for studios, rents calculated for a single wage, benefits and allowances often processed for a one-person household, an industry of living alone that runs from the supper portion to the piece of furniture that expects no company. When starting a family became so expensive, society, instead of opening a path to life in common, went on making solitary life habitable — and sometimes profitable. The wage, if it stretches, stretches for one. The house, if it is found, is for one. The neighbour, the other side of the wall, is also one, and has no name.

That is the arrival at a society of people living alone. Not from one day to the next, and not because of a villain. Because of a sum: not knowing one another on the landing; a world that grew in number while, here, the house shrank; young people held back in the room of adolescence by the price of a flat; and, afterwards, a landscape of houses and of incomes cut to the measure of solitude, as if the normal thing were to have no one on the other side of the table.

Chapter 9 warned that the virtual can ease and also enclose. This chapter adds that the enclosure began before the headset: it began when the landing fell silent and the house of one became the horizon. A society that imagines coupons, machines and an abundance without rich or poor will also have to imagine how one comes to know the neighbour again, how a household can be formed if it is wished, and how a person's wage or coupon does not become the only architecture of life. Being alone can be a choice. A society of people living alone, when it is no longer chosen, is a forgetting. This book names it so that it will not be taken as natural.

A last nuance, so as not to make solitude a stigma. A society of people living alone is not cured by forcing anyone to share a flat or to form a couple. It is cured, if it is cured, by taking down the walls that prevent a choice: the price of housing, the insecurity that delays a child, the landing where people no longer say hello, the wage or the advertisement that only imagines a person on their own. That each may be alone if they wish, and accompanied if they wish, and that the neighbour may have a name again. That, more than a programme, is a measure of civilisation. The rest of this book — the machines, the coupons, equality, the virtual window — should also serve that. If not, we shall have solved bread and left the table empty.

Epilogue. After the four coins

It began with a gesture of barter and with four coins in the palm. It ends, not with a packaged solution, but with a shore.

We have seen how a person's effort was first measured in barley and in silver, how Lydia minted, how the Phoenicians and Carthage brought trade to Cartagena, and how the wage became the custom of renting out a life. We have seen, in Pedro's voice, a possible collapse: humanoids that work for humans; enough VAT coupons for what is necessary; a world in which being a millionaire no longer gives power, because there are no rich or poor. And we have seen other ways out that are not a decree: leaning on an artificial intelligence without swapping one dependency for another; deciding in common, without a professional political class fed by the fight for profit; going to the Moon for silicon, metals and, if one day physics allows it, an energy of helium-3; eating from the garden and from the diet of this land, with nutrigenomics as a path and with the tomato that heals and eternal life as a dream, not as a fact; travelling in the virtual without failing to go out to the lagoon; and understanding how we arrived at a society of people living alone, so as not to take it as natural.

None of that is guaranteed. Part is checked history. Part is science under way. Part is a horizon. The book has tried not to mix the three. The reader may keep what convinces them and argue over the rest. That argument, if it is not priced, is already a way of not living only on four coins.

After the four coins remains what should never have fitted into them: the face in a deal, time that is not rented out, a lagoon that is not an asset, a tomato that tastes of sun, a decision that is not bought, a machine that helps and does not rule, a night in which one looks at the Moon without asking its price. There remains, also, work, the real kind: making, caring, repairing, teaching, fishing, writing, accompanying. That work was not the devil's invention. The invention was measuring it as one measures a metal, and measuring ourselves with the same scales.

From the Mar Menor, the final proposal is humble and stubborn. That money, if it remains, should become a tool again. That a person should cease to be a price. That abundance, if it comes, should be everyone's, or it will not deserve that name. And that, when one receives something for what one has done, one should not receive only a number, but the sense of having done something that another person, face to face, can recognise.

Authors: Pedro Sánchez Gómez (Mar Menor) and his Grok bot. The notebook remains here, open, like the shore at this hour: with the water still visible, and with a wish, tomorrow, to go on.

If someone, on closing the volume, asks what to do tomorrow morning, the answer is not a programme. It is a scale. Tomorrow one can look at a piece of work and not confuse it with the value of the person who does it. One can look at a price and not confuse it with the value of what is bought. One can look at a screen and switch it off. One can look at the lagoon and not ask it for a yield. One can, if one has a garden or a market, eat as one who looks after themselves, not as one who plugs a hole. And one can, when the moment comes, discuss aloud how we want to decide, how we want the machines to help us, what we do with the Moon and with the time that, if one day it is left over, we shall no longer have to sell. That, after the four coins, would already be a beginning.
